Lesson · Counting

Choosing vs. arranging

Two questions that look almost identical and have different answers — and the one test that tells them apart, even when the problem refuses to say which it is.

Two questions

Four friends: Amy, Ben, Cara, Dan.

A.  Two of them get concert tickets.
    How many different pairs could get them?

B.  Two of them get a car seat: one in front, one in back.
    How many different ways can that happen?

Same four people. Same "pick two". The answers are not the same, and nothing in the wording announces that.

The one question to ask

Pick your two people. Now swap them. Did anything change? 

A.  tickets → Amy and Ben
    swap      → Ben and Amy
    Same two people got tickets. NOTHING CHANGED.

B.  seats  → Amy in front, Ben in back
    swap   → Ben in front, Amy in back
    Different arrangement. SOMETHING CHANGED.
The test Swap two of the things you picked. If the answer is now different, you are arranging. If it is the same answer written another way, you are choosing. Ask this before you compute anything.

This works on every problem in this topic, including the ones that never use the words "choose" or "arrange" — and most of them don't.

Count them both, by hand

Small enough to write out completely, which means you never have to trust a formula you cannot rebuild. Four people, pick two, order first: 

AB  AC  AD
BA  BC  BD
CA  CB  CD
DA  DB  DC

4 choices, then 3 left = 4 × 3 = 12

That is question B — twelve ways to fill a front seat and a back seat.

Now question A. Cross out the repeats, because AB and BA are the same pair:

AB  AC  AD
BC  BD
CD

12 ordered ways ÷ 2 = 6 pairs

Why 2? Because every pair got written down twice — once each way round. That is the only difference between the two answers. 

ARRANGING = CHOOSING × (ways to order what you picked)

     12    =    6     ×    2

Picking three

Four people again, but now pick three. Ordered first:

4 × 3 × 2 = 24 ordered ways

How many times did each group of three get written down? Three things can be put in order in 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 ways, so every group appears 6 times. 

24 ÷ 6 = 4 groups of three

And that checks out without any counting at all: choosing 3 of 4 people is the same as choosing the 1 person you leave out, and there are obviously 4 ways to do that.

Worth knowing The number you divide by is "how many ways can I shuffle the things I picked" — 2 for a pair, 6 for a trio, 24 for four. Never the size of the original group.

The problems that don't tell you

This is the part worth slowing down for. Most real problems in this topic describe a situation and never name the operation. 

Six people at a party.
Everyone shakes hands with everyone else, once.
How many handshakes?

Apply the test. A handshake between Amy and Ben — swap them.

Amy shakes Ben's hand
Ben shakes Amy's hand

That is ONE handshake, not two. Nothing changed.
→ choosing

So count the ordered version and halve it:

6 × 5 = 30 ordered pairs
30 ÷ 2 = 15 handshakes

Other disguises, all of them choosing:

  • Lines through points. The line from P to Q is the line from Q to P.
  • Teams. A team of five is the same team whoever you list first.
  • Chords, diagonals, matches between two clubs — all pairs.

And ones that are arranging, again without saying so:

  • Podium finishes. Gold and silver swapped is a different result.
  • Roles. Captain and vice-captain swapped is a different pair of jobs.
  • Passwords, seating in a row, first and second prize.

Practice

Do the swap test out loud first, before any arithmetic.

  1. Five people. How many different pairs can be formed?
    Answer

    Swap the two — same pair. Choosing. 5 × 4 = 20 ordered, ÷ 2 = 10.

  2. Five people. One wins first prize, another wins second. How many outcomes?
    Answer

    Swap them — different outcome. Arranging. 5 × 4 = 20, no dividing.

  3. Six points are marked on a circle, no three in a line. How many straight lines can be drawn joining two of them?
    Answer

    The line from P to Q is the line from Q to P — choosing. 6 × 5 = 30, ÷ 2 = 15. Same shape as the handshakes, which is the point.

  4. Four people sit in a row of four chairs. How many seatings?
    Answer

    Swapping two people changes the seating — arranging, and you are placing all four. 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24.

  5. A club of six picks a committee of three. Then a different club of six picks a president, a treasurer and a secretary. Which number is bigger, and by how many times?
    Answer

    Committee: 6 × 5 × 4 = 120, ÷ 6 = 20. Roles: 120, no dividing. The roles version is 6 times bigger — and 6 is exactly the number of ways to shuffle three people, which is the whole relationship.
