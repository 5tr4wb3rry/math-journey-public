Choosing vs. arranging — the half that hides

You already know the swap test: pick your things, swap two of them. If the answer changes, you are arranging. If it stays the same, you are choosing. That test works on every problem — including the ones that never say "choose" or "arrange". This lesson is about spotting the choosing problems when they wear a disguise.

The problem: some problems never say "choose"

A problem says "committee" — you know it is choosing. A problem says "captain and vice-captain" — you know it is arranging. But most real problems describe a situation and name neither operation:

  • Nine people at a dinner. Every pair toasts once. How many toasts?
  • A pizza place offers fourteen toppings. How many different two-topping pizzas can you order?
  • Thirteen players enter a round-robin chess tournament. Every pair plays one game. How many games?
  • A bookshelf holds eleven titles. You are taking three for the trip. How many different sets of three?

All four are choosing problems. None uses the word "choose". The skill is recognising that before you do any arithmetic.

The recognition move

When a problem does not tell you what it is, ask two questions out loud:

  1. What is being picked? List the actual objects — people, toppings, games, books, lines, handshakes, teams.
  2. Does the order I list them matter? Swap two of the picked things. Is it a different outcome? That is the swap test you already own.

If swapping changes nothing — the pair {Amy, Ben} is the same pair as {Ben, Amy}; the pizza with pepperoni and mushrooms is the same pizza — you are choosing. Count the ordered version, then divide by the number of ways to shuffle what you picked.

Disguises that are always choosing

These descriptions always mean choosing — they never mean arranging. Learn to recognise them on sight.

  • Pairs / handshakes / toasts / matches / lines / chords / diagonals. A handshake between A and B is one handshake, not two.
  • Teams / committees / groups / sets / subsets. A team of three is the same team whoever you list first.
  • Connections / friendships / edges / roads between two places. The road from X to Y is the road from Y to X.
  • Toppings on a pizza / ingredients in a mix / flavours in a combo. Pepperoni + mushroom = mushroom + pepperoni.
  • Books for the trip / cards in a hand / numbers in a combination lock (ironic name).
The common thread Every one of these describes an unordered set. Swapping two members of the set never produces a different set. That is the definition of choosing.

Once you recognise it: how to count it

You have already done the hard part — recognising it is choosing. The arithmetic is the same pattern every time:

  1. Count the ordered version: multiply down from the total, one factor per thing you are picking.
  2. Divide by the number of ways to shuffle what you picked: k! for k things. Pairs divide by 2, trios divide by 6, etc.

Nine people at a dinner. Every pair toasts once.

What is picked? A pair of people.
Swap them — same pair. Choosing.
Ordered picks: 9 × 8 = 72
Each pair counted twice: 72 ÷ 2 = 36 toasts

Eleven books on the shelf. Pick three for the trip.

What is picked? A set of three books.
Swap two books — same set. Choosing.
Ordered picks: 11 × 10 × 9 = 990
Each set counted 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 times: 990 ÷ 6 = 165 sets

Seven players in a round-robin tournament. Every pair plays one match.

What is picked? A pair of players for one match.
Swap them — same match. Choosing.
Ordered picks: 7 × 6 = 42
Each match counted twice: 42 ÷ 2 = 21 matches
The pattern Both steps are mechanical once you have recognised choosing. The only step that requires thinking is step 0: "What is being picked, and does order matter?" Everything after is the same every time.

Practice

Do the swap test out loud first, before any arithmetic.

  1. Ten people at a party. Everyone shakes hands with everyone else, once. How many handshakes?
    Answer

    What is picked? A pair of people shaking hands. Swap them — same handshake. Choosing. 10 × 9 = 90 ordered, ÷ 2 = 45.

  2. A club has twelve members. They need to pick a committee of four. How many different committees?
    Answer

    A committee is a set — order does not matter. Choosing. 12 × 11 × 10 × 9 = 11880 ordered. Each committee counted 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24 times. 11880 ÷ 24 = 495.

  3. Six points are marked on a circle, no three in a line. How many straight lines can be drawn joining two of them?
    Answer

    The line from P to Q is the line from Q to P. Choosing. 6 × 5 = 30, ÷ 2 = 15. Same shape as handshakes and toasts.

  4. A sandwich shop offers nine fillings. How many different 3-filling sandwiches can you make?
    Answer

    A sandwich with ham, cheese, and lettuce is the same sandwich with lettuce, cheese, ham. Choosing. 9 × 8 × 7 = 504, ÷ 6 = 84.

  5. Eight people. How many different pairs can be formed?
    Answer

    Pairs are choosing. 8 × 7 = 56, ÷ 2 = 28.
