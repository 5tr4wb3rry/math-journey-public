Lesson · Counting factors and divisors

Counting things you can't list

Some questions ask how many of something there are, where writing them all out would take a week. The trick is always the same: break the number into primes first, and count from there.

Start here

100! means 100 × 99 × 98 × … × 2 × 1. It is a colossal number — 158 digits long. It ends in a run of zeros. 

How many zeros does 100! end in?

You cannot multiply it out. You cannot look at it. And yet the answer is findable exactly, with no calculator, in about a minute — because you do not need the number, only one fact about it.

Where zeros come from

A number ends in a zero when it is divisible by 10. It ends in two zeros when it is divisible by 100, three when divisible by 1000, and so on. So the question is really: how many times does 10 divide into 100!?

And 10 is not prime. 10 = 2 × 5. Every zero at the end needs one 2 and one 5. 

zeros = how many (2 × 5) pairs you can make

Now look at which is scarcer. Every second number contributes a 2. Only every fifth number contributes a 5. There are far more 2s available than 5s, so the 5s run out first — and the number of pairs is limited by the 5s alone.

The whole idea Counting the zeros of 100! means counting how many 5s appear in its prime factorisation. The 2s take care of themselves.

Working it out

Which numbers from 1 to 100 bring a factor of 5 with them?

5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, … , 95, 100

That is every 5th number:
100 ÷ 5 = 20 of them.

So there are at least 20 fives. But that count treats every one of those numbers as contributing a single 5, and some of them contribute more than one. 

25 = 5 × 5      brings TWO fives, not one
50 = 2 × 5 × 5  brings TWO fives
75 = 3 × 5 × 5  brings TWO fives
100 = 4 × 5 × 5 brings TWO fives

Each of those four numbers was counted once in the first sweep, so each still owes one more. Rather than patching them one at a time, notice what they have in common: they are exactly the multiples of 25. So sweep again. 

multiples of 5   →  100 ÷ 5  = 20
multiples of 25
  → 100 ÷ 25 = 4
  (each of these owed one more)
multiples of 125
  → 100 ÷ 125 = 0
  (125 > 100, so we stop)

total fives = 20 + 4 = 24
Easy to miss Stopping after the first sweep. Twenty is the count of numbers divisible by 5, not the count of fives. A number like 25 is one number but two fives, and the second sweep is what picks those up.

100! ends in 24 zeros.

A smaller one, same method

Zeros at the end of 50!

50 ÷ 5  = 10
50 ÷ 25 = 2
50 ÷ 125 = 0, stop

10 + 2 = 12 zeros

The same idea, different question

That was counting how many times one prime divides a number. The other half of this topic is counting all the divisors of a number — and it runs on the same engine: break it into primes first. 

How many divisors does 720 have?

Break it down:

720 = 2 × 360 = 2 × 2 × 180 = … = 2⁴ × 3² × 5¹

Now think about what any divisor of 720 must look like. It can use zero, one, two, three or four 2s — five choices. Zero, one or two 3s — three choices. Zero or one 5 — two choices. Every combination gives a different divisor, and every divisor arises exactly once. 

2⁴ → exponent 4 → 5 choices  (0,1,2,3,4)
3² → exponent 2 → 3 choices  (0,1,2)
5¹ → exponent 1 → 2 choices  (0,1)

5 × 3 × 2 = 30 divisors
The rule Add one to each exponent, then multiply. The "add one" is there because using none of a prime is a legitimate choice.

Quick check on something small enough to verify by hand:

36 = 2² × 3²  →  3 × 3 = 9 divisors
and indeed: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36  ✓

Your turn

  1. How many zeros does 30! end in?
    Answer7 — because 30÷5 = 6, and 30÷25 = 1, giving 6 + 1.
  2. How many zeros does 200! end in?
    Answer49 — 200÷5 = 40, 200÷25 = 8, 200÷125 = 1, then stop. 40 + 8 + 1.
  3. How many divisors does 1000 have?
    Answer16 — 1000 = 2³ × 5³, so (3+1)(3+1).
  4. How many divisors does 2⁵ × 7³ have?
    Answer24 — (5+1)(3+1). You never need the actual number, which is 10976.
  5. Harder: what is the smallest factorial that ends in at least 6 zeros?
    Answer25! — 25÷5 = 5 and 25÷25 = 1, giving 6. At 24! you only have 4, since 24÷5 = 4.
